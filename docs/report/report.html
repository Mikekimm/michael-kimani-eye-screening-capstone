Scope of claims. This is screening and referral support, not a diagnostic device. The models were trained and evaluated only on public datasets; there has been no field deployment, external validation or clinical validation. Every figure below comes from a recorded run in the repository.

1. Scope alignment and feature validation

The approved proposal planned an offline-first Android app that screens fundus photos for diabetic retinopathy (DR), glaucoma and cataract on the device, refers uncertain or high-risk cases to a specialist dashboard, and syncs when connectivity returns. Following supervisor feedback (a feasible prototype with one demonstrable workflow; reconcile offline use with sync and authentication; separate research evaluation from development), effort went first into one complete, tested workflow; the model registry then made adding the glaucoma and cataract models a data task with no code change. Main deviation: the proposal specified React Native with on-device TensorFlow Lite; the delivered app is an installable PWA with server-side inference (one code base for Android and iPhone, models verified and replaced on the server); offline therefore covers capture and queueing, and scoring happens after sync. Specialty routing (an extension beyond the proposal, which lists only a single Specialist role). The health worker ticks which conditions a question is about and chooses routine or urgent; specialists have specialties (DR, glaucoma, cataract, general) set by the administrator; the queue shows matching cases first and all cases on a second tab; "Take this case" assigns a case, others see it as taken, and the health worker sees "Being reviewed by" the specialist. Role checks are on the server; overdue cases are counted per priority.

Added beyond the proposal: sign-up requests approved by an administrator (below), a fundus check that refuses non-eye photos, Cancel / Analyse, Retake / Upload another and Save / Discard in the capture flow, removal of a screening with a reason, daily counts in the clinic's time zone, a SHA-256-verified model registry whose cards state each model's resize, eye laterality and model version on every result, an append-only review log and an audit log.

How access is controlled. Sign-up is a request: the person gives only name, email, password and the role they request (health worker or specialist; admin cannot be requested). Until an administrator acts, sign-in with the correct password returns "waiting for administrator approval" (a wrong password gets the generic message); the requested role grants nothing. A second sign-up with a registered email is refused and changes nothing. The administrator approves with the requested or another role, optionally recording the facility, or rejects with a reason that is emailed; every decision is audited. The app does not verify anyone's identity or qualifications; the administrator is expected to check outside the app. Every API request is checked on the server (route guards and one AccessPolicy); users cannot change their own role; sign-in and sign-up are rate-limited; passwords use Argon2id with rotating JWT refresh tokens.

2. Technical functionality

Frontend. A React 18 + TypeScript (strict) PWA, mobile-first (bottom tabs below 1024 px, sidebar above). Pages read server data only through hooks that call one typed API client, which attaches the JWT, refreshes it once on a 401 for all concurrent requests, and converts failures into plain-language messages. Health workers register patients by clinic code, choose the eye, and capture a photo with a circular guide (rear camera or gallery); on-device hints flag dark or blurry photos before upload.

Backend. FastAPI with a layered design: routers (HTTP and role guards) → services (use cases) → repositories (all SQL). POST /api/screenings checks type, size and that the bytes really decode as JPEG/PNG, applies the quality gate and a fundus check (blank, tiny or non-eye photos return 422 with a reason and nothing is saved), then runs every model the registry has verified and maps each score through that condition's risk policy. One diagnosis_results row is stored per condition with its model version, including "not available" rows. A ReferralAdvisor turns the results into one recommendation. As in the proposal's sequence diagram, an uncertain case is flagged and queued automatically (borderline results and usable photos with quality warnings; configurable); a clear positive shows "Refer recommended" and the health worker sends it with "Ask a specialist"; clear negatives are not queued. Severe results (DR band 4-5) default to urgent. Review is asynchronous from a shared queue: one specialist with the "general" specialty sees every case, and a district specialist can serve several clinics. Specialists do not prescribe: they approve, override with a reason, request a retake or comment; the app supports screening and referral only. The health worker's home counts new decisions until they are opened; the queue, home and history refresh every 30 seconds. SMS or email notifications are future work. The specialist review is a later confirmation, not a gate: the health worker gives the recommended action from the result at the same visit, and the review confirms or corrects it afterwards. Review is asynchronous, as in the proposal: the case waits in the queue with a review target (urgent 24 h, routine 72 h; configurable; my design decision, not a proposal requirement) and overdue cases rise to the top. Specialists approve, override any scored condition (reason required), request a retake (shown as "Retake needed") or comment; each action is appended to review_events, which the ORM refuses to update or delete, and reaches the health worker on the next sync. Each result card shows the model's measured limits; results made by an earlier model are never re-scored and carry a notice instead.

Offline and sync. Without a connection, photos and new patients are stored in IndexedDB with a client-generated UUID. A sync engine pushes them in order (patients first), retrying with bounded exponential backoff and full jitter; because the server is idempotent on the UUID, a retry after a lost reply returns the original record rather than a duplicate. Each cycle then pulls specialist decisions made since the last pull. Offline mode captures and queues; it does not run the model on the device.

Data handling and privacy. SQLite by default, PostgreSQL by configuration, schema managed by Alembic migrations. Patients are pseudonymous codes; images are stored under random names and served only to authorised users; analytics and audit records contain no images or patient fields; secrets live only in a git-ignored .env generated by a script.

Different inputs: negative, borderline and positive scores, real and synthetic fundus photos, non-eye images, blank, tiny, corrupt, mislabelled, oversized and future-dated uploads, invalid patient data, weak passwords, expired and replayed tokens, and every role against every route. Fundus check: on 25 real fundus photos and 77 non-eye images it wrongly refused 3 of 25 fundus photos and accepted 0 of 77 non-eye images; it was tuned on these same images and no face photos were tested, so the figures are optimistic. Improvements made because of testing: the supervisor's live test showed a screenshot accepted as an eye and every positive result sent automatically, which led to the fundus check as a hard stop and the "Ask a specialist" rule; a form test exposed that the password field dropped its form reference, so sign-in could never succeed (fixed with forwardRef); an end-to-end script test exposed that the calibration script did not write its outputs (fixed); screenshots led to listing DR first and to plain-language referral reasons; Lighthouse raised SEO from 91 to 100; axe-core found unlabeled camera inputs and, after the new models, low-contrast amber badges (both fixed); the parity test showed the app was resizing every photo with nearest-neighbour, which would have changed 6 of 24 DR v2 decisions, so each model card now states its resize.

Analysis: inference is a small share of the round trip, and limiting it to one thread costs about 5 ms, so a modest server CPU is sufficient. On a slow phone and network the wait rises to about 4 s (2.7 s before the routing changes), dominated by the upload; the offline queue means the worker can continue with the next patient meanwhile. Low-end figures are emulated (Chrome DevTools); physical low-end devices and real iOS Safari have not been tested.

DR v2 is MobileNetV2 fine-tuned from block_13_expand on the Kaggle EyePACS + APTOS + Messidor redistribution, split 70/15/15 by image group; the threshold was chosen on validation (highest with sensitivity ≥ 85%) and the test split scored once. Its figures are a held-out test split by image (no patient IDs available; near-duplicates could not be excluded); not external validation; not clinically validated: the notebook found as many groups as images, so augmented copies may sit in both training and test. Every figure was recomputed from the notebook's predictions with the app's own threshold and bootstrap code and matched; the served file has the notebook's checksum, and on 24 of its test photos the app reproduces the notebook's scores within 0.021 (tolerance 0.03, my choice) with no decision changed. Glaucoma and cataract (ODIR-5K) were split by patient; ODIR labels come from free-text keywords and its positive classes are small. For reference only, Gulshan et al. (2016) reported 90.3% / 98.1% on different data and a high-specificity operating point; our referable-DR (grade 2+) sensitivity is 0.869. The earlier DR model (v0.3) reached only 0.542 sensitivity on its validation fold.

  • Specificity below target means about 25% of eyes without DR are referred, and about 9 in 10 glaucoma flags are false alarms; the result screens say so next to each result, and a specialist reviews every referral.
  • The fundus check is a heuristic: 3 of 25 real fundus photos were wrongly refused and no face photos were tested.
  • Not done: on-device inference in the browser, a load balancer, a usability study with health workers (SUS kit ready), tests on a physical phone (planned on an iPhone 13 Pro after the HTTPS deploy), external validation and smartphone-lens images.
